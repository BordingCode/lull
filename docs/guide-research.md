## 1. Safe sleep

### Back to sleep
- [DK] "Always put your infant to sleep on its back." Stomach and side sleeping "greatly increase" the risk of cot death. Once a baby can roll over by itself, including in its sleep, it may stay on its tummy. A baby may sleep on its tummy on a parent's chest only if the parent is awake. (SST-VD-parents)
- [DK] Stomach sleeping carries up to 10× the risk of back sleeping, and side sleeping about 2×. If a baby spits up, it turns its head by reflex, and there is no evidence of extra choking risk on the back. (SST-VD-prof)
- AAP: side sleeping is as unsafe as stomach sleeping. Once a baby rolls both ways comfortably, you don't need to roll it back. (AAP-parents)
- NHS: put the baby "feet to foot" (feet at the bottom of the cot, Moses basket or pram). (NHS-SIDS)
- [DK] The three core rules since 1991: (1) back sleeping, (2) no nicotine products in pregnancy (cigarettes *and snus*) and no tobacco smoke after birth, (3) avoid overheating. Cot deaths in Denmark fell from about 120 a year to about 5 or fewer a year. (SST-VD-parents)

### Surface and "nothing in the bed"
- [DK] Use a fairly firm mattress that reaches the edges of the bed. No pillow. No loose blankets, toys, cloth nappies or similar. Place the baby near the foot end. Cot bars no more than 7 cm apart. (SST-VD-parents; SST-VD-prof)
- [DK][DIFF] **Denmark recommends a light baby duvet** ("en let babydyne, der passer til sengen, og som barnet kan sparke af sig"). No junior duvet in the first year. (SST-VD-parents; SST-SundeBørn p.67)
  - AAP: no blankets or other soft bedding. Use layered clothing or a wearable blanket (sleep sack) instead. (AAP-parents)
  - UK: avoid duvets and quilts. Use a lightweight sleeping bag or a light sheet or blanket. (Lullaby-temp; NHS-SIDS)
  - SST does not mention sleeping bags at all.
- [DK] SST advises against lambskin as an indoor sleep surface. It also advises against pillows, wedges or positioners sold to prevent a flat head. (SST-VD-prof)
- AAP: the surface must be firm and flat. Any incline over 10° is unsafe. No weighted sleep sacks, swaddles or blankets. (AAP-parents)

### Room-sharing
- [DK] "The safest place for an infant to sleep is in its own bed in the same room as the parents. This applies especially in the first 6 months, but preferably throughout the first year." (SST-VD-parents; SST-VD-prof)
- AAP 2022: room-share for **at least** the first 6 months. The AAP says this can lower SIDS risk by up to 50%. (AAP-parents)
- NHS: same room for every sleep for at least the first 6 months. (NHS-SIDS)
- [DIFF] The difference is small, but Denmark explicitly prefers the whole first year.

### Bed-sharing
- [DK][DIFF] **Denmark discourages bed-sharing (since 2011) but gives harm-reduction rules for parents who choose it.** The AAP's wording is stricter. SST's rules (SST-VD-prof; SST-VD-parents; SST-SundeBørn p.19–20):
  - The baby sleeps **beside one parent and next to its own cot, never between the parents**, and must not be able to fall into the gap.
  - It has its own thin baby duvet and as much free space as in its own cot.
  - The parents' bed is at least 140 cm wide (at least 180 cm if a parent has obesity). The mattresses are firm and close together; one big mattress is best.
  - Never on a sofa, sofa bed, daybed, waterbed or narrow hospital bed.
  - No siblings or pets in the bed.
  - Never bed-share if a parent is very tired, ill, has taken sedating medicine or has drunk alcohol.
  - After a feed, put the baby back in its own place.
  - The risk is highest in the first 4 months, and when the mother used nicotine in pregnancy, a parent smokes, a parent is affected by alcohol or medicine, or the baby sleeps with a non-parent or with siblings.
- AAP 2022: "avoid bed sharing under any circumstances", including with twins. The risk is especially high with alcohol, cannabis, sedating drugs, smoking or vaping, a baby under 4 months, or a premature or small baby. If you might fall asleep with the baby in your bed, clear away pillows and blankets first. (AAP-parents)
- NHS: a separate cot is safest. Bed-sharing is unsafe if the baby was born before 37 weeks or weighed under 2.5 kg, or if a parent smokes, has had 2 or more units of alcohol, or has taken drugs or drowsy-making medicine. (NHS-SIDS)
- Sofas and armchairs are flagged as high-risk by DK, AAP and NHS alike.

### Smoking
- [DK] Exposure to nicotine in pregnancy plus smoke after birth multiplies the risk 2–4×. If both parents smoke, the risk doubles. Bed-sharing is riskier when parents smoke. Snus in pregnancy is also a risk (Swedish data). (SST-VD-prof)
- [DK] Airing out or using the cooker hood does not remove smoke toxins. Never smoke indoors. (SST-SundeBørn p.25)
- AAP: no smoking or vaping near the baby, even outdoors. Keep the home and car smoke-free. (AAP-parents)

### Overheating
- [DK] The baby must not be sweaty while asleep. Check the **neck or front of the chest**. Never pack the baby tightly in duvets or blankets. Always take outdoor clothes off when you come inside, even if the baby is asleep, including in a pram, carrycot (lift) or snowsuit bag. A baby with a fever should wear light clothes and sleep under just a sheet. (SST-VD-parents; SST-VD-prof)
- AAP: dress the baby in one layer more than an adult needs to be comfortable. Watch for sweating, a hot chest or flushed skin. No hats indoors. (AAP-parents)
- UK: take hats off indoors and in cars, buses and trains, even if this wakes the baby. (NHS-SIDS; Lullaby-temp)

### Pacifier (dummy, "sut")
- [DK][DIFF] **Denmark does not actively recommend a pacifier for SIDS prevention.** SST says there "may possibly" be a protective effect and some countries include it in their advice, "but the evidence is not strong" and the mechanism is unclear. (SST-VD-prof)
- [DK] *Sunde børn* says you can "consider" a pacifier once breastfeeding is well established, as a comfort tool. With breastfeeding problems it says to limit or avoid the pacifier. (SST-SundeBørn p.74, p.43)
- AAP: **offer** a pacifier at naps and bedtime, even if it falls out once the baby is asleep. If breastfeeding, wait until breastfeeding is well established. Don't force it. Never hang it around the neck or clip it on during sleep. (AAP-parents)
- NHS: "consider" a dummy, since some research suggests it may lower SIDS risk. (NHS-SIDS)

### Swaddling
- AAP: swaddling does not reduce SIDS risk. Always place a swaddled baby on its back. Don't wrap too tightly or restrict the hips. **Stop when the baby shows signs of trying to roll**, usually around 3–4 months and sometimes earlier. (AAP-parents)
- Meta-analysis: swaddling raised SIDS risk most when the baby slept prone (odds ratio 12.99) or on its side (3.16), and less on the back (1.93). Limited evidence suggests the risk rises with age, with about double the risk after 6 months. (Pease et al. 2016, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27244847/)
- NHS: don't swaddle when bed-sharing. Sleeping bags are safer. (NHS-SIDS)
- [DK] SST's leaflets don't address swaddling. *Sunde børn* mentions a "svøb" (wrap) or blanket only as a way to calm a crying baby, adding that you should loosen the duvet once the baby sleeps so it doesn't overheat. (SST-SundeBørn p.74–75)

### Sitting devices and car seats
- [DK] Use the car seat only for transport. Small babies don't do well sitting up for long. SST advises against frequent use of car seats and bouncers outside transport. (SST-VD-parents; SST-VD-prof)
- AAP: if a baby falls asleep in a car seat, stroller, swing, carrier or sling, move it to a firm, flat surface on its back as soon as possible. Don't use products not made for infant sleep (e.g., nursing pillows, loungers). (AAP-parents)
- US death data: 3.0% of 11,779 sleep-related infant deaths happened in sitting devices. 62.9% of those were in car seats, and in under 10% of those cases the seat was being used as directed. Over half of the car-seat deaths happened at home. (Liaw et al. 2019, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31110162/)
- NHS: a baby may sleep in a car seat while travelling, but take it out once you arrive. For slings, follow T.I.C.K.S. (Tight, In view, Close enough to kiss, Keep chin off chest, Supported back). (NHS-SIDS)

### Prams and outdoor napping (a Danish norm)
- [DK] A baby can nap outside once it has **started gaining weight after birth and is otherwise thriving**. Dress for the weather and **check the temperature several times during the nap** by feeling the neck or chest. (SST-SundeBørn p.26–27)
- [DK] **Summer:** dress lightly, keep the pram in the shade and move it if the shade moves. Use an insect net ("myggenet"). **Don't cover the opening with a cloth nappy**, because it limits airflow and lets the inside heat up. (SST-SundeBørn p.27)
- [DK] **Winter:** insulate the bottom and sides of the pram (e.g., with newspaper), dress the baby warmly, use a warm duvet, and park the pram out of the wind. Outdoor sleep is **discouraged below −10 °C and in freezing fog or misty weather** ("rimtåge eller diset vejr"). (SST-SundeBørn p.27)
- [DK] **Rain:** park the pram under a roof and leave the rain cover off. Use a rain cover only while walking, and only an approved one that fits the pram. A loose elastic can let the baby pull the cover in and suffocate. (SST-SundeBørn p.27)
- [DK] Indoors (e.g., in a shopping centre), loosen the duvet and/or take outdoor clothes off. (SST-SundeBørn p.27)
- [DK] Children sleeping outside in a pram or cot must be **checked regularly**, with or without a harness. "No harness can be considered completely safe." If a harness is used, it must be a sleep harness meeting EN 13210 (VAREFAKTA-marked), fastened tight to the bottom of the pram, and checked for wear every time. Deaths happen almost every year in carrycots, cots and prams, from vomit, duvets, entanglement or blocked airways. Check more often in wind, low temperatures, early illness, or if the child seems unusually passive or restless. (SST-Seler; written for daycare but the safety facts apply generally)
- [DK] A "kattenet" (cat net) is not mentioned in the SST material I found. SST says "myggenet" (insect net). Don't claim SST recommends cat nets.
- Research: in a Finnish study of 3-month-olds, outdoor winter naps lasted **92 minutes longer** than indoor naps, but got shorter as skin cooled faster. Cold extremities were sometimes seen. (Tourula et al. 2010, Acta Paediatrica, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20377534/). This is observational only; claims that outdoor naps prevent illness are not established.
- [DIFF] The NHS and AAP give no outdoor-napping guidance; it isn't a norm there.

## 2. Room environment

### Temperature
- [DK][DIFF] **Denmark: 18–20 °C where the baby sleeps**, never above 20 °C. If you open a window to keep it below 20 °C, keep the baby out of the draught. (SST-VD-parents; SST-VD-prof; SST-SundeBørn p.67 says "max. 20 degrees", p.70 says "about 18–20")
- UK: **16–20 °C**, checked with a room thermometer. If the heating is on overnight, set it no higher than 20 °C. (Lullaby-temp; NHS-SIDS)
- AAP: gives no number. Dress the baby one layer more than an adult and watch for signs of overheating. (AAP-parents)
- [DK] General indoor temperature for the home is 20–22 °C. Air out twice a day for 5–10 minutes and always before bedtime. (SST-SundeBørn p.25)

### Clothing and bedding
- [DK] Don't overdress for the night. Use a light baby duvet that the baby can kick off. Avoid clothes with cords. Choose breathable cotton or wool. (SST-SundeBørn p.26, p.67)
- AAP and UK: use a wearable blanket or sleeping bag rather than loose bedding (see section 1).

### Darkness and light
- [DK] Give the baby daylight during the day and dim the lights before bedtime. At night use as little light as possible; a red bulb is suggested as "less stimulating". (SST-SundeBørn p.68, p.70)
- In 6–12-week-old babies, more light in the early afternoon was linked with better night sleep. This is observational and suggests that ordinary daylight helps the body clock develop. (Harrison 2004, J Sleep Res, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15560769/)
- NHS: daytime means open curtains, play and normal household noise. Night means low light, quiet voices and no play. (NHS-sleep)

### White noise
- [DK] *Sunde børn* describes a monotone, faint hum (a vacuum cleaner, a hairdryer, "white noise") as calming. (SST-SundeBørn p.74, p.53)
- Hearing safety: 14 infant sleep machines were tested at **maximum volume**. At 30 cm, all of them exceeded **50 dBA**, the recommended limit in hospital nurseries. Three exceeded **85 dBA**, which over more than 8 hours would exceed adult workplace limits and risk noise-induced hearing loss. The levels were also measured at 100 and 200 cm. (Hugh et al. 2014, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24590753/)
- Practical advice from that work: place the machine **as far from the cot as possible (not on the rail or in the cot)**, keep the volume low, and run it for a limited time. (Hugh 2014, recommendations section; summarised by CHOP: https://www.chop.edu/news/white-noise-not-right-noise)
- No source found that shows white noise improves infant sleep over the long term. Keep claims modest.

## 3. Sleep by age

### Official totals per 24 hours (including naps)

| Age | DK / SST 2024 | WHO 2019 | AASM 2016 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 mo | 14–17 h (11–13 h or 18–19 h can be fine; not valid for the first days) | 14–17 h | No recommendation (normal range too wide) |
| 4–11 mo | 12–15 h (10–11 h or 16–18 h can be fine) | 12–16 h | 12–16 h (4–12 mo) |
| 1–2 y | 11–14 h (9–10 h or 15–16 h can be fine) | 11–14 h | 11–14 h |
| 3–5 y | 10–13 h | 10–13 h (3–4 y) | 10–13 h |

- Sources: SST-Søvnlængde, which builds on the US National Sleep Foundation figures and WHO; WHO 2019 (https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more); Paruthi et al. 2016, AASM consensus (https://depts.washington.edu/dbpeds/pediatricsleepdurationconsensus.pdf).
- [DIFF] For 4–11 months, Denmark says 12–15 h while WHO and AASM say 12–16 h.
- [DK] A child who seems fresh and rested during the day is getting enough sleep. (SST-SundeBørn p.67; SST-Søvnlængde)
- **Variation is large.** A meta-analysis of 34 studies gave a mean of 12.8 h for infants, with a normal range of **9.7–15.9 h**. Night waking ranged from 0–3.4 times per night at 0–2 months to 0–2.5 times at 1–2 years. (Galland et al. 2012, Sleep Med Rev, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21784676/)

### Per age band
Where no source gives figures for a narrower band, the wider official band applies. Treat these as typical, not as targets.

- **Newborn (0–4 weeks):**
  - Sleep: about 15–18 h, in stretches of 2–4 h around the clock (some babies 16 h, others 20 h). No day/night rhythm; sleep follows hunger and fullness. (SST-SundeBørn p.11–12, p.68; SST-Søvnlængde)
  - Night: at least 8 feeds per 24 h are needed early on. Once the baby is gaining weight, you no longer need to wake it at night. (SST-SundeBørn p.11–12, p.53)
  - Other figures: NHS says about 18 h; AAP says about 16–17 h, often only 1–2 h at a time. (NHS-sleep; https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/getting-your-baby-to-sleep.aspx)
  - Sleep often begins in REM (active) sleep until about 2–3 months. (Grigg-Damberger 2016, JCSM, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26951412/)
- **2 months:** around 6 weeks to 2 months, babies begin to tell day from night and sleep more at night. They can sleep about 3 h at a time. (SST-SundeBørn p.53, p.68)
- **3 months:** a 24-hour rhythm starts forming at 1–3 months. (SST-Søvnlængde)
  - The biggest growth in self-regulated night sleep happens between 1 and 4 months.
  - By 3 months there was a 50% chance of sleeping 00:00–05:00 or 8 h straight, but this is not universal. (Henderson et al. 2010, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20974775/)
- **4–5 months:**
  - Daytime: 3–4 sleeps. Night: often up to 4–5 h in a stretch. Total about 14–15 h (SST's book figure; its 2024 range is 12–15 h).
  - Most babies can "sleep through", which SST defines as about 5 h. Breastfed babies usually have slightly shorter stretches. (SST-SundeBørn p.68)
  - The rhythm becomes more stable. (SST-SundeBørn)
- **6 months:** night sleep consolidates into one main period at 6–9 months. (SST-Søvnlængde)
  - Even so, **28–57% of 6- and 12-month-olds did not sleep 6 or 8 h straight**, with no link to development or maternal mood. (Pennestri et al. 2018, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30420470/)
  - NHS: some babies sleep 5–8 h or more from 3–6 months, but not all. (NHS-sleep)
- **6–12 months (7–8, 9–10, 11–12):**
  - Naps: 3 at first, then 2. Near 1 year, perhaps about 6 h straight at night. Total about 14–15 h (SST-SundeBørn p.68).
  - A 2-nap pattern was well established by 9–12 months. (Weissbluth 1995, Sleep, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7792496/)
  - **78.6% of 6–12-month-olds still woke at least once a night**, and 61.4% had at least one milk feed. Both fell with age. Breastfeeding versus formula made no difference to waking. (Brown & Harries 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25973527/)
- **13–15 and 16–18 months:**
  - 1–2 naps, total 12–14 h. Morning and afternoon naps early in this period; from about 1½ years, one nap, typically after lunch. (SST-SundeBørn p.69)
  - One afternoon nap typically by 15–24 months. (Weissbluth 1995)
  - Restless periods at night are common, e.g., when teeth come through. (SST-SundeBørn p.69)
- **19–24 months:** usually 1 nap. Total 11–14 h by official ranges. (SST-Søvnlængde; WHO; AASM)
- **2–3 years:** 1 nap.
  - Most children stop napping between 3 and 5 years (SST: this is partly cultural). Once the nap goes, the child needs more night sleep and often an earlier bedtime. (SST-Søvnlængde)
  - Most children still napped in their 3rd and 4th year; naps usually disappeared by 7. (Weissbluth 1995)
  - NHS: 2 years about 12–14 h including naps. (NHS-sleep)

### Wake windows: no official standard
- Wake windows are **not** part of SST, AAP, NHS, WHO or AASM guidance.
- A pediatric sleep physician found zero PubMed references. He says the numbers "do not seem to be based on any scientific evidence" and the concept isn't taught in pediatric sleep medicine, though sleep pressure itself is real. (Canapari, https://drcraigcanapari.com/wake-windows)
- A typical practitioner chart, as reproduced there (not his own):

| Age | Wake window |
|---|---|
| Newborn | 60–90 min |
| 4–6 mo | 1.5–2.5 h |
| 7–9 mo | 2–3.5 h |
| 10–12 mo | 2.5–4 h |
| 13–15 mo | 2.5–4.5 h |
| 16–18 mo | 4.5–5.5 h |
| 18 mo+ | 5–6 h |

- **If Lull shows wake windows, label them "practitioner rule of thumb, not research-based"** and put the baby's own sleepy cues first.
- [DK] SST's own wording is "put the baby down when it is tired but not overtired", plus fed, calm and secure. (SST-SundeBørn p.67)

## 4. Common problems

### Fights naps / won't nap
- **Normal:** needs vary widely (Galland 2012). Total daytime sleep is a fairly stable trait of the individual child from 6 to 18 months. (Weissbluth 1995)
- **To try:**
  - A short version of the bedtime routine before naps; watch for sleepy cues; don't panic during nap strikes. (Canapari)
  - [DK] Check the basics first: fed, dry nappy, comfortable clothes, room about 18–20 °C, enough daytime sleep. (SST-SundeBørn p.70)
- **Red flags:** see section 6. [DK] Talk to the health visitor (sundhedsplejerske) if sleep is persistently hard. (SST-SundeBørn p.69)

### Frequent night waking
- **Normal:** most babies 6–12 months still wake (Brown & Harries 2015). 28–57% don't do 6–8 h straight at 6–12 months (Pennestri 2018).
  - [DK] "At this age 'sleeping through' usually just means 5–6 hours." (SST-SundeBørn p.71)
  - AAP: a 6-month-old normally wakes and resettles within minutes. (healthychildren, getting-your-baby-to-sleep)
- **To try:**
  - A consistent bedtime routine (section 5).
  - [DK] Calm daytime feeds, so a distracted baby doesn't make up its milk at night. Don't overload the last evening meal; it can upset the tummy.
  - [DK] Keep the same daily rhythm on holidays and weekends. During teething, illness or the start of daycare, give comfort but keep the routine.
  - [DK] Parents moving around in the same bed or room can wake the baby. (SST-SundeBørn p.71)
- **[DK][DIFF] Night feeds:** SST says "from about 6 months you can try dropping the middle-of-the-night feed. The baby no longer has a physical need for food at night." Suggested tactics: the father handles nights, or the mother sleeps elsewhere for a while. (SST-SundeBørn p.71)
  - The NHS is softer: for *some* babies aged 6–12 months night feeds may no longer be needed. (NHS-sleep)
  - Recommend Lull presents this as an option, not a rule.
- **Red flags:** fever, pain, snoring or breathing pauses, poor weight gain (section 6). [DK] Never give over-the-counter painkillers without a doctor's advice; they can hide symptoms. (SST-SundeBørn p.71)

### Early morning waking
- No research-based guidance found specifically for infants. Only general measures are supported: daylight in the daytime, dim evenings, a regular rhythm (SST-SundeBørn p.70; Harrison 2004).
- Don't present specific "fix early waking" schedules as evidence-based.

### Bedtime resistance
- **Evidence:** behavioural treatments for bedtime problems and night waking (consistent routines, gradual approaches) have the strongest support in ages 0–5. 94% of 52 studies found them effective. (Mindell et al. 2006, AASM review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17068979/; Morgenthaler et al. 2006 practice parameters, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17068980/)
- **Bedtime fading** means temporarily moving bedtime later, to when the child actually falls asleep, then gradually earlier again. In a randomised trial it cut the time to fall asleep with no stress effects. (Gradisar et al. 2016, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27221288/)
- [DK] Wind down in good time. No wild play right before bed. (SST-SundeBørn p.70)

### Short naps
- No guideline defines a "too short" nap. Normal variation is large (Galland 2012).
- Sleep architecture changes over the first months: sleep often starts in REM until about 2–3 months (Grigg-Damberger 2016), and more adult-like organisation develops afterwards (Bathory & Tomopoulos 2017 review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28117135/).
- I found **no authoritative source for the "45-minute cycle"** figure that apps often quote. Avoid stating an exact cycle length.
- Outdoor pram naps ran longer in a Finnish study (Tourula 2010).

### Dropping a nap (typical ages)
- 3 → 2 naps: during 6–12 months (SST-SundeBørn). 2 naps were well established by 9–12 months (Weissbluth 1995).
- 2 → 1 nap: from about 1½ years (SST-SundeBørn p.69); typically 15–24 months (Weissbluth 1995).
- 1 → 0 naps: most children stop between 3 and 5 years (SST-Søvnlængde). Usually gone by 7 (Weissbluth).
- [DK] When the nap goes, move bedtime earlier. (SST-Søvnlængde)

### "Sleep regressions"
- **The fixed-date regression calendar** (4, 8, 12, 18 months, and so on) is not in SST, AAP, NHS or AASM guidance. My PubMed / Europe PMC searches found no peer-reviewed study establishing regressions at fixed ages. Sources that use the term are blogs and sleep-consultant material.
- **What is real:**
  - Big developmental changes in sleep across 0–6 months: a 24-hour rhythm forms at 1–3 months and night sleep consolidates at 6–9 months (SST-Søvnlængde).
  - The fastest consolidation happens in the first 4 months (Henderson 2010).
  - Sleep often starts in REM until 2–3 months (Grigg-Damberger 2016).
  - So a change in sleep around 3–4 months is plausible as **maturation of sleep architecture**, but not as a predictable "regression" with a set start and end.
  - [DK] SST attributes temporary unrest to identifiable things: teething, illness, starting daycare, moving, new impressions. "Often the problem is temporary." (SST-SundeBørn p.70–71)
- **Teething:** in a prospective study of 475 tooth eruptions, symptoms clustered in an 8-day window (4 days before to 3 days after the tooth appeared). "Wakefulness" was more common in that window, but "sleep disturbance" was not significantly linked. No symptom reliably predicted a tooth. (Macknin et al. 2000, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10742315/)
- **Suggested framing:** "Sleep often changes when babies develop, get ill, or their routine changes. These phases are common and usually pass. Fixed regression ages aren't backed by research."

### Overtiredness
- [DK] SST uses the idea: put the baby down "tired but not overtired". (SST-SundeBørn p.67) Clinically, a tired baby can be harder to settle; that much is ordinary practice.
- **The "overtired → cortisol surge → fights sleep" mechanism is not established.** I found no controlled study showing that a normal delay in sleep floods an infant with cortisol. Studies on sleep and cortisol in children are correlational. (Reviewed via searches; e.g., Canapari's critique, https://drcraigcanapari.com/the-sleep-edit-19-debunking-bad-online-sleep-advice/)
- Cortisol in the sleep-training debate:
  - Middlemiss 2012 found raised infant cortisol on day 3 of an inpatient extinction programme. It was small (n=25) and had no control group. (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21945361/)
  - The randomised trial by Gradisar 2016 found **small-to-moderate cortisol decreases** with graduated extinction and bedtime fading compared with controls.
- **Recommendation:** say "a very tired baby can find it harder to settle". Don't make hormone claims.

### Day/night confusion (newborns)
- **Normal:** newborns have no day/night rhythm. Babies start to tell day from night around 6 weeks to 2 months. (SST-SundeBørn p.53, p.68)
- **To try:**
  - [DK] Minimal light at night (red bulb), no talking during night feeds and changes, change the nappy only if it's dirty or very wet. (SST-SundeBørn p.68)
  - NHS: daytime light, play and normal household noise. (NHS-sleep)
  - AAP: lively days, calm nights. (healthychildren)
  - Daytime light exposure is linked with better night sleep. (Harrison 2004)

### Daylight saving time
- Denmark follows EU clock-change dates: the last Sunday of March and the last Sunday of October (EU Directive 2000/84/EC, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/84/oj).
- I found **no peer-reviewed actigraphy study of infants or toddlers** around clock changes. Common advice is to shift bedtime in 10–15-minute steps over the days around the change, or to shift the whole day at once and use morning daylight. This is practical advice, not tested.
- [DK] SST's general advice, to keep the daily rhythm and use daylight, applies here. (SST-SundeBørn p.70–71)

## 5. Gentle, evidence-based settling

- **Bedtime routine (best-supported, lowest-risk measure):**
  - A randomised trial in children aged 7–36 months: a consistent nightly routine for 2 weeks cut the time to fall asleep and the number and length of night wakings, and improved mothers' mood. (Mindell et al. 2009, Sleep, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19480226/)
  - In 10,085 children aged 0–5 across 13 countries, a more consistent routine went with an earlier bedtime, faster sleep onset, fewer wakings and longer sleep, in a dose-dependent way. The more nights with a routine, and the earlier it was started, the better. (Mindell et al. 2015, Sleep, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25325483/)
  - [DK] SST recommends a cosy routine of **no more than 10–15 minutes**, e.g., tooth-brushing, a story and the same lullaby each night, with light, sound and activity turned down well before bed. (SST-SundeBørn p.67)
- **[DK] SST's settling stance (responsive and gradual):**
  - From about 3–4 months, begin separating feeding from falling asleep. (SST-SundeBørn p.57)
  - From 4–5 months: do the routine, put the baby in its bed, sit beside it and say you'll stay until it sleeps. If it gets upset, put a calming hand on its tummy, rock it, or pick it up until calm and lay it down again. **Stay in the room** so the baby isn't confused. (SST-SundeBørn p.57)
  - Later: comfort in steps, from words, to words plus holding in the cot, to picking up, while staying in the bedroom. (SST-SundeBørn p.61)
  - After the routine, parents may sit holding the baby's hand until it sleeps, or leave and let the baby fall asleep alone. SST says to choose what suits the family and the child. (SST-SundeBørn p.67)
  - [DK] Crying is not a sign of being "spoiled"; the baby "has something to tell you". (SST-SundeBørn p.67) Danish babies cry about 2 h a day on average; SST suggests babies in some countries cry more because they're left to cry before being picked up. (SST-SundeBørn p.75)
  - **SST does not describe cry-it-out or timed check-ins.**
- **Graduated extinction ("controlled comforting") and bedtime fading:**
  - RCT, ages 6–16 months: both improved sleep. Graduated extinction cut the number of wakings. There was no rise in cortisol, and at 12 months no difference in emotional or behavioural problems or attachment. (Gradisar 2016)
  - 5-year follow-up of a population RCT (intervention at 8–10 months): **no lasting harms or benefits** at age 6 for emotions, behaviour, stress, the parent–child relationship or maternal mental health. (Price et al. 2012, Pediatrics, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22966034/)
  - **Not before about 6 months:** the trials enrolled babies of 6 months and older. A systematic review found that behavioural sleep interventions in the first 6 months don't improve outcomes and may risk more crying, earlier weaning, more maternal anxiety, and higher SIDS risk if the baby sleeps in a separate room. (Douglas & Hill 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24042081/)
  - Australian researchers recommend controlled comforting only after 6 months. (The Conversation, https://theconversation.com/mondays-medical-myth-controlled-crying-damages-babies-brains-11665)
- **Unmodified extinction** (leave and don't return) is rated effective by AASM (Morgenthaler 2006). It conflicts with SST's "stay in the room" approach, so if mentioned in a Danish app it should be noted as not part of SST's advice.
- **[DK] Never shake a baby.** If a parent can't cope with crying: lay the baby in its cot, leave the room, breathe, and return when calm. (SST-SundeBørn p.75)

## 6. Red flags: seek medical help

- **Emergency (call 112 in Denmark; 911 is the US equivalent):**
  - Breathing stops for more than 20 seconds.
  - Skin turns pale, bluish or grey.
  - A change in muscle tone (floppy or stiff).
  - Source: healthychildren, https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/pages/Sleep-Apnea-Detection.aspx
- Danish acute-care advice: seizures, a rash that doesn't fade when pressed, a stiff neck, or a floppy baby who is hard to make contact with means call 112. (Summary of sundhed.dk / regional guidance; see https://www.sundhed.dk/borger/patienthaandbogen/boern/sygdomme/infektioner/feber-hos-boern/)
- **Fever under 3 months:**
  - [DK] Contact a doctor. SST says even a moderate rise in temperature in babies under 3 months can be a serious sign, and acutely ill babies in their first year should be seen by a doctor on broad indications. (SST, *Vurdering og visitation af akut syge spædbørn*, https://www.sst.dk/media/bqmfkfff/akut_syge-d-pdf.pdf)
  - sundhed.dk lists fever under 3 months as a danger sign. Babies under 6 months are hard to assess, so contact a doctor whenever you're worried. (sundhed.dk link above)
- **Normal vs not:**
  - Periodic breathing (short pauses followed by catch-up breaths) is generally normal and usually outgrown by about 6 months. (healthychildren)
  - Frequent snoring, breathing problems at night, or daytime sleepiness should be reported to the doctor. (healthychildren)
- **Contact the health visitor or GP:**
  - Persistent sleep difficulty. (SST-SundeBørn p.69)
  - Long bouts of crying: rule out pain or illness first. Colic is defined as 3 h of crying a day, at least 3 days a week, in the first 3 months, and it passes on its own. (SST-SundeBørn p.75)
  - Jaundice lasting more than 2 weeks: the baby must be examined, ideally in week 3. (SST-SundeBørn p.11–12)
  - A very sleepy baby with jaundice who must be woken for feeds. (SST-SundeBørn p.11–12)
  - A flat or asymmetric head. (SST-VD-parents)
- Poor weight gain, persistent breathing difficulty and other feeding issues should be raised with the health visitor or doctor. This is general advice; Lull should link to its feeding and growth sections.

## 7. Common baby-app claims that are not supported

| Claim | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| "Regressions happen at 4, 8, 12, 18 months" | No peer-reviewed support for fixed ages. Only developmental change and situational disruption are documented (section 4). |
| "Follow these wake windows" | No research basis; it's a practitioner rule of thumb (Canapari). |
| "Overtiredness floods the baby with cortisol" | Not demonstrated. In the best RCT, cortisol went down with sleep interventions (Gradisar 2016). |
| "Sleep training damages the brain or attachment" | No lasting harms at 6 years (Price 2012). No attachment differences at 12 months (Gradisar 2016). Caveat: these were babies of 6 months and older. |
| "Sleep training works for young babies" | No benefit shown under 6 months (Douglas & Hill 2013). |
| "Babies should sleep through the night by 6 months" | 28–57% don't at 6–12 months, with no developmental harm (Pennestri 2018). 78.6% still wake at 6–12 months (Brown & Harries 2015). |
| "Rice cereal in the bottle helps babies sleep" | RCT: no difference (Macknin et al. 1989, Am J Dis Child, https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/514762). [DK] SST: a big evening meal rarely helps and can disturb sleep (SST-SundeBørn p.71). Nuance: the EAT trial found early solids from 3 months gave about **16.6 more minutes** of sleep a night at 6 months and slightly fewer wakings (2.01 → 1.74) (Perkin et al. 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29987321/). A small effect, not a reason to start solids early. |
| "More daytime milk or food stops night waking" | It reduced night *feeds*, not night *wakings* (Brown & Harries 2015). |
| "Breastfed babies sleep worse" (6–12 mo) | No difference in wakings between breast- and formula-fed babies at 6–12 months (Brown & Harries 2015). Breastfed babies do have shorter stretches in early months (SST-SundeBørn p.68). |
| "Teething ruins sleep for weeks" | Symptoms cluster in about 8 days around eruption, and "sleep disturbance" was not significantly linked (Macknin 2000). |
| "White noise is safe at any volume / helps prevent SIDS" | Machines can exceed safe levels (Hugh 2014). No SIDS benefit is claimed by any authority. |
| "Monitors / smart socks prevent SIDS" | AAP: no evidence that home monitors reduce SIDS. They don't replace safe-sleep rules (AAP-parents). |
| "Swaddling reduces SIDS" | AAP: it doesn't. The risk rises when prone or side sleeping, and possibly with age (Pease 2016). |
| "Positioners or wedges prevent flat head" | SST advises against all such aids (SST-VD-prof). AAP: no inclined or non-sleep products. |
| "45-minute sleep cycle means short naps" | No authoritative source found for an exact figure. Avoid it. |
| "Keep the baby awake more in the day so it sleeps longer at night" | AAP's parent page does say this (healthychildren getting-your-baby-to-sleep). SST instead says ensure "not too much and not too little" daytime sleep. No trial found. Present it cautiously. |

## 8. Danish vs US/UK differences (summary)

| Topic | Denmark (SST) | US (AAP) / UK (NHS, Lullaby) |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature | **18–20 °C, max 20** | UK 16–20 °C; AAP no number |
| Bedding | **Light baby duvet** the baby can kick off | AAP: no blankets, use a sleep sack. UK: no duvets, use a sleeping bag or light blanket |
| Room-sharing | Especially 6 mo, **preferably whole first year** | At least 6 mo |
| Bed-sharing | Discouraged, but **harm-reduction rules** (beside one parent, own duvet, bed ≥140 cm, never between parents) | AAP: "avoid under any circumstances". NHS: lists when it's unsafe |
| Pacifier | Possible protective effect, "evidence not strong"; not actively recommended | AAP: **offer** at sleep. NHS: "consider" |
| Swaddling | Not addressed in SST's SIDS material | AAP: stop at signs of rolling (3–4 mo). NHS: not when bed-sharing |
| Outdoor pram naps | Normal practice; not below −10 °C or in freezing fog; check neck or chest; insect net; no cloth over the opening; check often | No guidance |
| Night feeds | "From about 6 months you can try dropping the night feed; no physical need" | NHS: may not be needed for *some* babies at 6–12 mo |
| Settling | Stay in the room, comfort gradually; no cry-it-out | AAP parent page: don't rush in from 4 months; AASM rates extinction methods effective |
| Lambskin | Advised against indoors | Not specifically addressed in the pages reviewed |
